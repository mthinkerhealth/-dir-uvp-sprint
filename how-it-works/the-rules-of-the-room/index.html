The Room Runs on Four Rules, Agreed to Before Anyone Speaks

Tom Barnes September 15, 2026

A UVP Sprint session runs on four fixed roles, and they’re stated out loud before anyone starts talking about the business.

One person runs the room. That person doesn’t decide what the company is; they rule on whether an answer holds up. If someone claims a differentiator, they get asked how a customer would see it, taste it, or check it for themselves. A claim that survives that question stays. A claim that doesn’t gets set aside, out loud, and the room moves on. Nobody wins a weak claim by repeating it louder.

The CEO is the only person who makes the calls on the business itself. Everyone else can argue and push back all day, but when the room reaches a decision it can’t settle with evidence alone, that decision belongs to the CEO, and it gets asked for directly: here’s the choice, which one do you pick.

The rest of the leadership team argues their part of the business, and they’re also the evidence for it. Nothing from outside overrules what the room already knows firsthand. A claim about the sales process gets tested against what the head of sales actually knows, not against a market report nobody in the room wrote.

A fourth role manages the mechanics: the clock, the record, who speaks next.

All four roles get set at the very start, and the room is asked, out loud, whether everyone agrees to run the day this way. That agreement is what makes it possible to spend the rest of the day on the business instead of on who gets to talk. The hard part happens once, before anyone has said anything substantive yet.

From there, the day runs on one repeating move. Someone makes a claim. It gets compressed down to what it’s really saying. Then it gets pushed: how would you show that, what’s the simplest version of it, how would a stranger know it’s true. If the answer holds, it stays. If it doesn’t, the room doesn’t force it through. There’s a specific point where the person running the room stops pushing and says plainly that a claim didn’t prove out today. That’s a real outcome, not a failure of the day. A UVP built on five true things beats one built on six, where the sixth is decoration.

One thing is off the table entirely: naming, taglines, branding, identity. Not because those don’t matter, but because they’re a different discipline with a different standard of proof, and mixing them into this room corrupts both. If the conversation drifts there, it gets redirected back, every time. The room stays credible by staying inside its own boundary, the same way a courtroom doesn’t rule on the defendant’s haircut.

By the end of the day, the room has a position every person in it was actually asked about, on the record, in some form. A UVP that sounds unanimous only because someone stayed quiet isn’t one, so nobody gets to stay quiet.

FAQ

Does everyone have to speak, or can quieter people opt out?
No. Every person in the room is drawn onto the record in some form, sometimes by speaking, sometimes in writing depending on the room. The form flexes; being asked does not.
What happens if the CEO and another leader disagree and time runs out?
The room doesn’t referee it forever. As the agenda’s time limit arrives, the person running the room turns the open question over to the CEO directly: have you heard enough, does this need more time, can we close it.
